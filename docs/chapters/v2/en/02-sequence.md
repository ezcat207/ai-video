# Chapter 2: Sequence — What You Show First, Then What

**Map position**: Principle 1 · Film = time + sequence.

Same face, cut to a bowl of soup, and viewers think "he's hungry"; cut to a coffin, and they think "he's grieving." The face never changed. The order changed, and the meaning flipped completely. That's the Kuleshov effect — the cheapest magic trick in film history.

![Match cut: pushing open an old wooden door, closing a modern glass door](images/ch04-matchcut.webp)

Look at this picture: a hand pushes open an old wooden door on the left, a hand closes a modern glass door on the right — two matching actions, cut together, and the viewer glides "smoothly" from one time and place to another. Get the order right, and two unrelated shots tell a story by themselves.

## What Sequence Is

**One line**: the order shots line up in. The director's first job: decide what the audience sees first, then what, and what it remembers last.

**For a 13-year-old**:

- **Order makes meaning**: A + B ≠ B + A. "Cat stares at the fish tank" → "cat pounces" is a hunt; "cat pounces" → "cat stares at the fish tank" is a memory. Same footage, swap the order, different story.
- **Three questions about order**: before you shoot, answer — ① what do I show first (hook) ② what next (buildup) ③ what do I leave last (ending)? Can't answer? Don't roll.
- **Juxtaposition is the rhetoric**: put two shots together and the audience automatically looks for the connection. A director's job isn't "explaining" — it's "placing side by side." Put two related shots together and the meaning grows on its own.

**Two cold facts**:
1. The brain is wired to find cause and effect — "A then B" gets read automatically as "A caused B." Order IS causality; get it wrong and the audience "misunderstands" your story.
2. The 15-second order template: 0–3 seconds hook (the loudest shot) → 3–12 seconds buildup (3 shots) → 12–15 seconds twist (1 shot). Learn the template first, style comes later.

## Try It on a 15-Second Short

For your 15 seconds, write the order before you shoot: which shot is the hook? Which 3 build it up? Who gets the twist? Order on paper means you won't end up with "a pile of footage and no idea which goes first" in the edit.

## Prompt Lesson: Write Order into the Shot List

An AI generates one shot at a time — order doesn't live in a single prompt, it lives in the **shot list**. Add one line at the end of every prompt: its "place in the order."

**Example (3 shots, one order)**:
- Shot 1 (first) Chinese: 猫的脸部特写，眼睛睁大，2 秒；顺序：第 1 个，钩子
- Shot 1 English: Close-up of a cat's face, eyes widening, 2 seconds; order: shot 1, hook
- Shot 2 (then) Chinese: 猫悄悄靠近一只老鼠玩具，镜头跟随，3 秒；顺序：第 2 个，发展
- Shot 2 English: Cat sneaking up on a toy mouse, camera following, 3 seconds; order: shot 2, buildup
- Shot 3 (last) Chinese: 猫一爪拍飞电视遥控器，得意地看镜头，3 秒；顺序：第 3 个，反转
- Shot 3 English: Cat swatting the TV remote away, smugly looking at camera, 3 seconds; order: shot 3, twist

**Key**: number every shot — generate 10 shots and they still won't get scrambled, because each one knows its place.

## Experiment You Can Repeat

Take 3 unrelated clips from your phone, arrange them in two different orders, add the same title. Watch: does swapping the order feel like swapping the story? One re-arrangement and sequence clicks.

## Blooper Warnings

- **Diary blooper**: filming from start to finish in time order, no hook, no twist — think of the ending first, then the opening.
- **Misunderstanding blooper**: wrong order makes the audience infer the wrong cause — check once: following this order, will viewers land on the meaning I want?
